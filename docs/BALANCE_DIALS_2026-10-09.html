The Balance Dials

What a competitive race series looks like, where ours misses, and which dials move it. A follow-up to the Balance Report.

Your point first

A king stat is good. We have an emperor.

You're right that some stats should just matter more. In college football a 95-speed receiver is a 95-speed receiver, and everyone knows it without being told. A clear "king stat" makes drafting legible and gives the game a fantasy: go get the fast one.

The problem today isn't that one stat leads. It's how much. Measure what decides a race, and Power plus Speed take 87% of it. Power alone, a car stat you draft once, takes 61%. Seven stats share under 2%. Those seven still appear on every card and every grade, so they are noise to the player.

Two different questions

Stat balance and competitive balance are separate dials

Stat balance

Inside one racer: which stats matter, and how much. This is your NCAA question. It shapes the draft, the cards and whether a grade means anything.

Competitive balance

Across the grid: how often the best team on paper wins. This is what makes a season fun to follow. A series can have a clear king stat and still be wide open.

They interact, but fixing one doesn't fix the other. The data below shows why.

Why a season is so predictable

one race: stats explain about half the order (ρ ≈ 0.7, so ρ² ≈ 0.5) the rest: dice (luck procs, mistakes, crashes, pit stops) 10 races: the dice average out; their spread shrinks by √10 ≈ 3.2× the stats don't shrink: they repeat every race so: a team that is slightly better on paper finishes ahead almost every time

This is the core of sports design. To keep a title open you can shrink the gaps between teams, add more dice per race, or add catch-up between races (packs already do this, but only for you). The stat tune shrank the gaps and the favourite rate fell from 74% to 54%. Halving the car spread fixed who gets the advantage (the draft slot), not how big the advantage is.

How I'd steer

Three dials, three targets

Each dial is one question with one number we can measure. The green band is my proposed target. These are opinions for us to argue about, not decisions.

Biggest wins, in order

Where I'd spend effort

  1. 1

    Make the car one great pick, not the title

    Shrink how far apart the rack cars roll, or how much a star is worth. It's the biggest single change: it evens the draft slots (2–30% becomes 6–18%), and it hands the king stat back to the drivers. Today 76% of a team is its car.

  2. 2

    Build a hierarchy: one king, a court, specialists

    Keep Speed first. Bring Aggression, Consistency, Acceleration and Grip up to "you notice them" (around 8–10% each). Then Tyre Mgmt, Endurance, Pit Crew and Luck become specialists (3–5% each), so every stat on a card means something. That gives drivers identities you can draft for: the qualifier, the brawler, the tyre whisperer.

  3. 3

    Then set the dice

    Once 1 and 2 are in, choose how often the favourite wins. We have dials for more dice per race (mistakes, safety cars, qualifying noise) and catch-up between races (packs for CPU teams too). This one is taste, so we choose it together: lower means more upsets, higher means the best team usually wins.

  4. 4

    Later: tracks crown different kings

    Once the court stats are alive, a track can promote one of them for the day (Consistency is king at the twisty track). That's how Speed can be king on average and still lose. It doesn't work today because the court is dead (Balance Report, Finding 2).

Where the numbers come from

Stat shares: the per-stat weights measured for the Balance Report (1,600 races, same-seed bumps), squared and normalised. Standings and favourite rates: the 200 seeded all-CPU seasons from the season read, with each team rated before race 1 from its raw stats. Car math: today's engine values.
